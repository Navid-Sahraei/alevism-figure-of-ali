3The Shahrbanu story: Iran meets Shiism

Shahrbanu, “Lady of the Land” (that is, of Iran), is said to have been a daughter of Yazdgird III (r. 632–651), the last Sasanian king. In the story, she is captured during the Arab conquest, brought to Medina, and becomes the wife of al-Husayn, son of Ali. Their son, Ali Zayn al-Abidin, is the fourth Imam. Every Twelver Imam from the fourth to the twelfth would therefore descend from both the Prophet’s family and the Sasanian royal house.

Pre-Islamic Iranian streamSasanian kingship and farr-i izadi (Middle Persian xwarrah), the divine royal glory carried by the dynasty.
Early Islamic streamThe Prophet’s family and the light of walaya, the sacred authority passed from Imam to Imam.
Yazdgird III → Shahrbanu ⚭ al-Husayn → Ali Zayn al-Abidin, 4th Imam

What the sources say. The oldest surviving accounts do not make her a princess. Ibn Sa‘d (d. 845) and Ibn Qutayba (d. 889) describe the mother of the fourth Imam as a slave from Sind, named Ghazala or Sulafa. The royal version appears in the late ninth century and grows in later Shiite works. The sources also give her many names: Shahrbanu, Shahzanan, Jahanshah, Salama, Sulafa, Ghazala. For these reasons the Encyclopaedia Iranica calls the Sasanian princess “undeniably legendary”.

Why it still matters. For Amir-Moezzi, the story is historically improbable but culturally decisive. It joins Qurayshi prophetic sanctity with Sasanian royal glory, and so gives the Imams a double legitimacy. It also opened Shiite spirituality to pre-Islamic Iranian motifs, and helped make Imami Shiism feel at home in Iran long before the Safavids made it the state religion.

A shrine near Tehran. On a hillside at Rey, south of Tehran, stands the shrine of Bibi Shahrbanu. According to its legend, after Karbala she fled to Iran; pursued by her enemies, she meant to cry “Ya Allah” but called out “Ya kuh!” (“O mountain!”), and the mountain opened to receive her. Traditionally only women and descendants of the Prophet may enter. Mary Boyce argued that the site was once holy to the Zoroastrian goddess Anahid (Anahita), and that her veneration continued here in Muslim form; a similar legend belongs to the Zoroastrian shrine of Banu Pars near Yazd.

4Short timeline

632 Death of the Prophet Muhammad; the succession crisis begins.
656–661 Ali’s caliphate; capital moved from Medina to Kufa; first civil wars (Jamal, Siffin).
680 Al-Husayn killed at Karbala.
685–687 Al-Mukhtar’s revolt in Kufa in the name of Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya (Kaysaniyya).
740 Revolt and death of Zayd ibn Ali in Kufa (Zaydiyya).
765 Death of Ja‘far al-Sadiq; Ismailis and Twelvers part ways.
874 / 941 Minor and Major Occultation of the twelfth Imam.
909 Fatimid (Ismaili) caliphate proclaimed in North Africa; Cairo from 973.
945 The Buyids take Baghdad; rise of rationalist Twelver theology (al-Mufid, al-Murtada, al-Tusi).
1090–1256 Nizari Ismaili state centred on Alamut.
13th c. Hacı Bektaş Veli in Anatolia; the later Bektashi order takes his name.
1501 Shah Ismail founds the Safavid state with Qizilbash support; Twelver Shiism becomes the state religion.
16th c. Ottoman persecution of the Qizilbash in Anatolia; centuries of secrecy follow.
Early 20th c. “Alevi” gradually replaces “Qizilbash” as the community’s name.
1989 Alevi revival in Germany (the Hamburg “Alevi Manifesto”); the cem becomes public.
2010 UNESCO inscribes the semah on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage.

5Who’s who

d. 661

Ali ibn Abi Talib

Cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet, fourth caliph, first Imam of the Shia; the “Figure of Ali” of this session.

d. 670

al-Hasan

Elder son of Ali and Fatima; second Imam. Ancestor of the Hasanids (Zaydi imams of Yemen, many sharifs).

d. 680

al-Husayn

Younger son of Ali and Fatima; third Imam, killed at Karbala. Ancestor of the Twelver and Ismaili Imams.

d. c. 700

Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya

Son of Ali by Khawla of the Banu Hanifa; focus of the Kaysaniyya and the first idea of a hidden, returning Mahdi.

d. 765

Ja‘far al-Sadiq

Sixth Imam and great teacher; the split between his sons Isma‘il and Musa divides Ismailis from Twelvers.

13th c.

Hacı Bektaş Veli

Anatolian saint, patron of the Bektashi order and central to Alevi devotion; often shown with a lion and a deer.

1487–1524

Shah Ismail I

Founder of the Safavid state, leader of the Qizilbash; his poems, under the pen name Khata’i, are still sung by Alevis.

b. 1956

Mohammad Ali Amir-Moezzi

Director of Studies Emeritus at EPHE (Paris); leading historian of early Shiite esotericism and of the Figure of Ali.

6Ali’s descendants, simplified

Which line of Ali’s family did each branch follow? Green: Husaynids; yellow: Hasanids; outlined: Ibn al-Hanafiyya’s line. “…” marks omitted generations.

  • Ali ibn Abi Talib
    • Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya → Abu Hashim Kaysaniyya
    • al-Husayn → Ali Zayn al-Abidin
      • Zayd ibn Ali (d. 740) → Yahya (d. 743) Zaydiyya
      • Muhammad al-Baqir → Ja‘far al-Sadiq
        • Isma‘il → Muhammad ibn Isma‘il → hidden imams → Fatimid caliphs → Nizari imams (Alamut, Aga Khan) Ismailiyya
        • Musa al-Kazim → Ali al-Rida → Imams 9–11 → Muhammad al-Mahdi (occultation 874/941) Twelvers
    • al-Hasan
      • al-Hasan al-Muthanna → Ibrahim → … Ibrahim Tabataba → al-Qasim al-Rassi → Zaydi imams of Yemen (897–1962) Zaydiyya
      • al-Hasan al-Muthanna → Abd Allah al-Mahd → Muhammad al-Nafs al-Zakiyya (revolt, d. 762)
      • Zayd ibn al-Hasan → … → al-Hasan ibn Zayd, Zaydi state in Tabaristan (864) Zaydiyya

The esoteric orders (Nusayris, Druze, Alevis and Bektashis, Yarsan) do not follow a single line of Imams. Simplified after F. Daftary, “ʿAlids”, Encyclopaedia of Islam THREE (2014); dates added from standard reference works.

7Five branches at a glance

Kaysaniyyalate 7th c.

Around Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya. First ideas of occultation (ghayba), return (raj‘a) and the promised Mahdi.

Zaydiyya8th c. to today

Armed revolt instead of taqiyya; any learned and brave descendant of Fatima who rises can be Imam. Yemen today.

Ismailiyya8th c. to today

An institutional batin and philosophical ta’wil; the Fatimid caliphate and Alamut. Today Bohras and Nizaris (Aga Khan).

Twelvers (Imamiyya)10th c. to today

After the Occultation, a turn to law and rationalism; Safavid state religion from 1501. Iran, Iraq, Lebanon.

Esoteric movementsmedieval to today

Nusayris (Alawites), Druze, Alevis and Bektashis, Yarsan: Ottoman records called them all ghulat; Mir-Hosseini shows their living “inner truth”.
